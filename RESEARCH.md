# Student problem search

**Status:** Still ideating. [Pasa!](#candidate-pasa) and [REmento](#sixth-pass---remento-campus-event-candidate-2026-10-25) are candidates with different levels of detail. No problem or prototype has been chosen.

## Reusable 25-minute brief

Copy this section for another audience or problem. Keep the first pass under 30 minutes: **5 minutes** to list candidates, **10 minutes** to check evidence and existing solutions, **10 minutes** to decide and define a demo.

- **Date / audience:**
- **Three candidate problems:**
  1. Problem: who experiences it, when, and the cost.
  2. Problem: who experiences it, when, and the cost.
  3. Problem: who experiences it, when, and the cost.
- **Evidence links:** For each candidate, link a direct study, observed user account, or primary source; state exactly what it supports.
- **Cross-domain seam:** Name the two domains and the moment when combining them changes a student's action.
- **Current workarounds / existing solutions:** What people use today and where it helps.
- **Nearest combined competitor:** Search for an existing product that already joins the two domains.
- **Strongest counterevidence:** A source or observation suggesting the problem is small, already solved, or unsuitable for this audience.
- **Build effort:** Smallest useful demo and its main dependency or risk.
- **Chosen problem:** One sentence naming the student, situation, and difficulty.
- **Decision:** Why this problem now; why current workarounds fall short; what the demo proves; what remains untested.
- **Next prototype task:** A specific build slice with visible completion criteria.
- **Two classmate questions:** Ask about recent behavior and where the current approach fails before showing the demo.

## First pass - 2026-10-25 (rejected)

This pass led to Deadline Detective. The user rejected it as too generic. Its evidence remains below as a record, but its decision and prototype task are superseded by the second pass.

**Audience:** Fellow college students managing several classes. Published evidence below comes from other institutions; local frequency and willingness to use a new tool are untested.

| Candidate problem | Evidence | What students do today / existing solutions | Strongest counterevidence and build effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Find and verify assignment deadlines** when dates are in a syllabus, LMS assignments, announcements, or other course pages. | A [2024 survey of 324 students](https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/sees_articles/658/) found that deadlines were the most important syllabus item, yet most respondents checked four LMS locations for them. An [Emerson College support case study](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2023/2/a-student-centered-approach-to-faculty-training-using-the-lms-to-foster-students-time-management) describes students hunting through Canvas because some assignments did not appear in its calendar or had unclear dates. | Students check the LMS and syllabus, then transfer dates to a planner. This is a crowded area. **Effort:** low for a paste-and-review demo; real LMS integrations or reliable image extraction would be larger. |
| **B. Know who owns each handoff in a group assignment** before one person has to rescue the project. | A [review of 57 relevant papers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10038701/) identifies uneven participation and unclear roles as recurring challenges. | **C. Locate the right course material** when an LMS course is inconsistently organized. | In a [qualitative study of postgraduate LMS use](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8573481/), participants said document labels and placement affected how easily they found materials. ### Retired decision

**Choose A:** A student with several courses spends time checking multiple course locations to confirm what is due and when. The strongest direct evidence is the 2024 survey and Emerson's observed support cases. We do **not** yet have evidence that our own classmates have the same frequency of trouble.

**Current workaround and gap:** Students check a syllabus or LMS, use the LMS calendar or to-do list where dates are populated, and copy anything missing into a personal planner. This can fail when instructors place dates in different locations or leave calendar entries incomplete, as described in the [Emerson case study](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2023/2/a-student-centered-approach-to-faculty-training-using-the-lms-to-foster-students-time-management). Existing planners are substantial competition. Our hypothesis is that a **source-linked review step** for missing or conflicting dates is useful enough to justify a separate tool; that hypothesis is untested. The prototype must never silently choose one of two conflicting dates.

**Small demo:** "Deadline Detective" lets a student paste course snippets, keeps the source text or URL beside each extracted date, and flags a missing or conflicting date for confirmation. The satisfying moment is resolving a suspect date into a clear weekly list. This demonstrates the source-review workflow, not a claim that extraction or reminders outperform existing planners.

**Untested assumptions:** Classmates encounter this often enough; they will paste course text into a tool; source-linked review is more useful than their current calendar; course rules permit pasting the material. Ask classmates before expanding scope, using sample or permissioned text for the first demo.

### Retired prototype task

Build a local browser demo with three fixtures: (1) one clear deadline, (2) two different dates for the same assignment, and (3) an assignment with no date. Let the user paste snippets with a course and source label/URL, parse explicit dates, show the exact source line beside each proposed deadline, and require a manual decision for conflicts or missing dates. After confirmation, show a seven-day list in **Asia/Manila** time. A demo is complete when all three fixtures can be reviewed end to end and no uncertain date is added without confirmation. Start with text input and local state; integrations can follow classmate feedback.

### Two classmate questions

1. "Think of your most recent assignment deadline: where did you first see it, and did you check another place to be sure?"
2. "In the last month, did any assignment have a missing or changed date? Walk me through what you did to confirm it."

AccessPulse was grounded in a real problem but called for facility data and accessibility validation beyond the desired first build. The third pass below supersedes it.

**Brief:** Find a problem with a concrete student action at the intersection of two domains, not a broader planner, map, or chatbot. Six combinations considered: food access + timetable; commute + timetable; coursework + job evidence; emergency advisories + timetable; campus accessibility + facility operations; campus accessibility + class locations. The last two combine into the selected direction below.

| --- | --- | --- |
| **Food access + class gaps:** a student has 30 minutes and a tight meal budget. | A [UP Diliman study of 100 undergraduates](https://digitalarchives.upd.edu.ph/item/63731) found that affordability, proximity, and time affect food choices; a [UPLB survey of 104 students](https://www.ukdr.uplb.edu.ph/etd-undergrad/12765/) also found serious food access concerns in its sample. [Pratt's official guidance](https://www.pratt.edu/administrative-departments/engagement-culture-community/accessibility-at-pratt/elevator-status/) explicitly tells students to contact facilities and support services if an outage affects their route to class. | [MIT's accessibility map](https://campusplanning.mit.edu/highlights-and-initiatives/accessibility-mapping-at-mit/) shows routes; [Pratt's elevator page](https://www.pratt.edu/administrative-departments/engagement-culture-community/accessibility-at-pratt/elevator-status/) shows status and last verification time. Those are meaningful solutions. In the sources checked, they do not connect a disruption to the affected student's next class and the specific action to take. **Other two combinations screened:** A [commute-and-timetable app at NUS](https://terminus.rcn.sh/) already tells students when to leave for class; [InTransparency](https://www.in-transparency.com/en) already connects coursework artifacts to employer-facing skill evidence. Both show why combining two domains alone is insufficient differentiation.

### Current choice: AccessPulse

**Problem:** A student who needs a step-free route can discover too late that the lift or entrance needed for the next classroom is unavailable. The cost can be a missed class, an unsafe detour, or a last-minute accommodation request. The evidence establishes that this failure occurs on some campuses, **not** that it occurs at FEU Tech.

**Domain seam:** Facilities operations know *what is unavailable*; the timetable knows *who needs which classroom next*. AccessPulse matches a verified disruption to a student's next class and shows the source, time last checked, and who to contact for an alternate arrangement. Its first useful output is an **affected-class alert**, not a general campus map. A route should only be suggested when its accessibility data is verified; an unknown route stays unknown.

**Current workaround and counterevidence:** A student checks a campus map or outage page, reasons through the route, then contacts facilities, disability support, or the instructor. [Pratt](https://www.pratt.edu/administrative-departments/engagement-culture-community/accessibility-at-pratt/elevator-status/) and [MIT](https://campusplanning.mit.edu/highlights-and-initiatives/accessibility-mapping-at-mit/) already provide parts of this workflow. We have found no evidence yet that FEU Tech publishes the data needed for a live version or that students there want this interface. Co-design with students who rely on accessible routes is necessary before claiming local fit.

**Small demo:** On a clearly fictional campus, select a class schedule and a route requirement, then toggle a lift or entrance outage. The next-class card changes from "route available" to "access uncertain" and shows the affected class, the incident source, its verification time, and a prefilled request for an accessible alternative. A second fixture has an unrelated outage and leaves the class unchanged. A third has stale or unverified data and refuses to claim the route is safe. The interaction is visual and immediate without treating disability as a game.

**Next prototype task:** Build a local browser demo with a tiny fictional building graph, two class locations, and three incident fixtures (relevant, irrelevant, stale). Match affected routes to the next class deterministically; show source and checked-at time; keep unknown access distinct from confirmed access; let the student copy an accommodation request. Do not use real campus names or imply live safety coverage. The demo is complete when all three fixtures produce the expected class card and an unverified route never receives a safe label.

**Riskiest assumption / first validation:** Students at our campus experience disruptions that can be reported accurately enough for this workflow. Show the fictional demo to at least one student who uses accessible routes and a campus facilities or support contact. Ask: "Tell me about the last time a lift, entrance, or route changed your way to class. How did you find out and whom did you contact?" Then ask: "Which part of this alert would you trust, and what information would it need before you acted on it?" Do not collect disability details or publish a student's schedule in the prototype.

## Third pass - Clucky-scale concepts, 2026-10-25 (candidates, no selection)

**Reference:** The user meant [Clucky's rooster alarm](https://tryclucky.com/): one clear problem, a memorable character, and a tiny mission that must be completed before the loop ends. Here the challenge is to borrow that product shape, not the alarm or rooster.

| Concept and two domains | The one repeatable interaction | Evidence and closest alternative | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hatch:** assignment initiation + two-minute missions. | Enter an avoided assignment; complete one visible two-minute starting action to hatch an egg. | [College procrastination research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3177330/) supports the problem. [Goblin Tools](https://goblin.tools/) already breaks tasks down, and [Stupid Small](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.stupidsmall.app) already delivers two-minute steps. | Simple, but the core mechanic is occupied. |
| **Peep:** help-seeking + rehearsal game. | Tell a character what you tried and rehearse one question before opening an office-hours message. | A [study of 39 students](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1443781.pdf) found anxiety and uncertainty around office hours. [Finta](https://finta.ai/blog/office-hours-preparation-ai) already offers AI-assisted question preparation. | Useful, but the added mission may be too slight to beat a short worksheet. |
| **Pasa!:** relay-race handoff + student group work. | Pass a draft/link to a named teammate; the receiver must tap **Got it** or **Blocked**. | A [review of online group projects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10038701/) found unclear roles and late participation. A [student-led STEM study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1614627/full) describes unanswered group messages and duplicated effort. [Glidely](https://glidely.site/students) already provides a board, owners, chat, and checklists. | A possible playful, narrow demo; group adoption and differentiation remain untested. |

### Candidate: Pasa!

**Problem:** In a group assignment, "I finished my part" can sit in chat without a clear receiver. The next teammate may not know the file is ready, may lack what they need, or may start duplicate work. Evidence from other universities supports this pattern; its frequency among our own classmates is untested.

**Domain seam:** In a relay race, a leg is complete only when the baton reaches the next runner. Pasa! applies that rule to a project deliverable. A sender chooses a teammate and attaches a link or short note. The next person explicitly accepts it or reports a blocker. The parcel character moves only on a confirmed handoff; a blocker leaves it visibly waiting. The playful motion communicates actual project state.

**Current workaround and counterevidence:** Students post "done" and a file link in group chat, then follow up. A shared task board such as [Glidely](https://glidely.site/students) already makes owners and progress visible. A [construction workflow product](https://baton.build/features.html) shows that explicit handover and acceptance are an established coordination pattern in another domain. Our bet is that a single, satisfying handoff is easier for a student group to adopt than maintaining a full board. We have not tested that bet.

**Small demo:** Three illustrated teammate avatars and one animated parcel. Switch between sender and receiver views on one device. The sender adds a sample slide-deck link and passes it. The receiver taps **Got it** or **Blocked** and gives a short reason. The group view shows exactly who holds the parcel and whether the handoff was accepted. One handoff, one visible outcome.

**Possible prototype task if chosen:** Build a one-screen local browser prototype with three sample teammates and two fixtures: accepted handoff and blocked handoff. Store state locally for the demo. Show deliverable link, sender, receiver, handoff time, and status. The parcel must stay with the sender until the receiver accepts; a blocked response must show the reason to everyone. Keep the first slice to the handoff loop and mascot animation. After this works, a shared link can make the interaction real across devices.

**Test with classmates:** Ask a group that recently submitted a project: (1) "When someone said their part was done, how did the next person know it was ready and usable?" (2) "Show me a handoff that stalled; would one-tap acceptance or a blocker message have changed what happened?" Success is a group recognizing a real stalled handoff and wanting to try the loop on its next project, not merely liking the character.

## Sixth pass - REmento campus-event candidate, 2026-10-25

**Status:** Detailed candidate, not a selected build. The proposed [PRD](PRD.md) and [design brief](REMENTO-DESIGN.md) define a possible hackathon prototype. Research below describes a plausible problem and real competitors; it does not establish that FEU Tech classmates need or want this product.

**Specific moment and two domains:** At a campus event, a student has just met someone and is about to move to the next activity. The two domains are **light event missions** and **contact memory**. REmento would turn that moment into a small action: note one detail about the person, exchange a way to reconnect with permission, and later see that encounter in a private event recap. The name is a working title.

**Problem hypothesis:** Students may leave a busy event with photos and a few new social connections but no reliable reminder of who they met or what they discussed. The cost is a missed or awkward follow-up. [UW–Madison's career center](https://careercenter.education.wisc.edu/prepare-apply/networking/) advises students to note what they learn and follow up after an event. [Cambridge Careers Service](https://www.careers.cam.ac.uk/networking) recommends keeping contact and conversation notes. These are practical guides, not measurements of how often local students forget a new contact. [A study of trade-show attendees](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15470148.2017.1308285) found follow-up with a minority of contacts in that setting; business-event attendees are not a proxy for our campus.

**Current workaround:** Take photos in the phone camera, follow or save someone in a social app, and put a name or conversation detail in Notes or contacts. [University of Edinburgh Careers Service](https://blogs.ed.ac.uk/careersinformed/how-to-network-with-ease/) advises making a note of whom you met and a memorable point afterward. This workflow may already be sufficient for classmates; the extra REmento steps could interrupt conversation.

| Existing product | Verified overlap | Plausible REmento test and counterevidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Mission Memories](https://www.missionmemories.app/about) | An organizer creates photo missions, guests join by link or QR, and each guest receives an Event Wrapped recap after close. Its current how-it-works page also describes mobile web participation. | REmento would prioritize a memory of a person and a private contact exchange over points and a shared gallery. Photo missions plus recap are **already combined**, so the recap alone is not a differentiator. |
| [Nalo Social](https://nalosocial.com/) | Guests check in, scan another person's badge to connect, add a note, and receive a next-morning list of people met with chosen contact details. | REmento would test whether four playful moments and a personal visual story fit a small campus event. Nalo already addresses meeting, remembering, and reconnecting, so an organizer may prefer its fuller event workflow. |
| [Remento](https://www.remento.co/) | A separate, established memory product uses spoken stories and QR-linked recordings in keepsake books. | The similar name is a launch risk, not a feature comparison. Public naming needs clearance or replacement. |

**Plausible gap, not a claim of novelty:** A short, mobile-web experience might be easier for students to try during a small event and might make one new connection easier to recall afterward. It must earn its extra interaction cost against camera + social apps and against Nalo Social. Stage 1's private on-device storage avoids a shared photo gallery, but changing devices can lose the recap. These are design hypotheses, not evidence of better outcomes.

**Small demo:** Prefill the Codex x UIAI Hackathon from the supplied deck. On two phones, join by link or event QR, capture an activity photo, write a text memory of a person (or add a consensual photo), scan the other person's combined social QR, then have an organizer close the event. Show one full and one partial private recap, and export a summary image that omits contact links and names by default. The deck's title slide lists **25 October 2026, 12:00–6:00 pm, Zone Offices BGC**; it gives no attendee total.

**Riskiest assumptions and first check:** (1) At least some classmates have recently wanted to reconnect with someone they met at an event and could not easily remember enough context. (2) They would complete a tiny note or QR exchange while still in the room. (3) A personal recap helps them remember the connection more than their current camera and social apps. Ask 3–5 classmates before showing the concept: “At the last campus event where you met someone new, what did you do to remember them and reconnect?” and “Was there anyone you intended to contact afterward but did not? What got in the way?” Record the recent example, current workaround, and whether the problem occurred. Then observe the proposed flow without coaching. Do not claim improved connections from enthusiasm for the visuals alone.

**Decision:** Keep REmento alongside the earlier candidates. Mission Memories and Nalo Social make the competitive bar high, while the absence of local interviews leaves demand unresolved. A prototype becomes the next task only if the user selects this direction.
